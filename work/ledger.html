Case study — Halcyon

Ledger
reconciliation engine

Halcyon's books closed at 02:00 and were readable at 08:00 — six hours of compute producing a number that was already stale, then corrected by hand. I replaced the batch with a streaming pipeline that settles in under two minutes.

01 — The brief Why a six-hour batch was a business problem, not a compute problem.

Every night at 02:00 a job woke up, re-read the entire day's transactions, re-matched them against four bank feeds and wrote a fresh settlement file. It ran until roughly 08:00. The result was correct when it landed and wrong by the time anyone opened it.

The match rate had plateaued around 99.7%. The remaining 0.3% went into a spreadsheet that two people corrected each morning — and because the batch rebuilt everything from scratch, every correction had to be re-derived the next night. Nobody trusted the number, so half the finance team kept their own.

The ask was "make the batch faster". The actual problem was that reconciliation was being treated as a report when it is a continuous process. Once you see it that way, the batch stops being the right shape.

02 — What I built An incremental matcher, an explainable rule engine, and a queue that drains itself.

Ledger processes entries as they arrive instead of re-reading the day. State is kept per account, so a match that completes today can be extended tomorrow without replaying history.

  • Deterministic rule engine — exact match first, then fuzzy candidates, then a confidence band. Every decision stores the rule that produced it, so "why did these pair?" is a query, not an investigation.
  • Exception queue for the 0.3% — keyboard-first triage with a one-click resubmit that writes back to the ledger and trains the next night's thresholds.
  • Seven-year audit trail — every match, override and source-feed version, retained and queryable. Finance stopped asking for exports.
  • Shadow backfill — fourteen months of history replayed through the new engine while the old batch kept running, so cutover was a configuration change rather than a weekend.

The interface is deliberately unglamorous: one screen, one queue, no dashboards to interpret. It was designed against the actual morning routine of the two people who used to own the spreadsheet.

03 — The hard part At-least-once delivery, order-dependent matching, and a cutover nobody could feel.

Replays have to be boring

Bank feeds re-deliver, reorder and occasionally contradict themselves. The matcher is stateful, so a naive replay double-counts. Every write is an idempotent upsert keyed on (feed, sequence, entry id) — reprocessing a window changes nothing, which is what made backfill safe to run twice.

Partial matches cross midnight

A settlement can be half-matched at 23:58 and completed at 00:04. State had to be sharded by account, checkpointed, and rebuildable without pausing intake — otherwise the fix for one edge case becomes a nightly outage.

Nobody adopts a number they can't check

The engine ran in shadow mode alongside the old batch for three weeks, diffing output line by line. We cut over only once it disagreed fewer than five times in a million — and when it did disagree, the audit trail explained the rule that caused it. That diff is what convinced finance, not the latency figure.

Settlement went from six hours to under two minutes. The compute bill for that path fell 94% because the nightly cluster was decommissioned entirely rather than resized.

The exception queue drains itself — zero manual corrections have been needed since cutover. The spreadsheet is gone, and the number the finance team reads at 09:00 is the same one the pipeline settled at 08:58.
